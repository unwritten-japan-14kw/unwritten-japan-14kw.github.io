<div class="scene" markdown="1">
The bank wants a Japanese phone number. The phone shop wants a Japanese bank account. You've been in Japan for five days, and you're standing between the two, holding a residence card that doesn't seem to open anything.
</div>

Almost every new arrival goes through this loop. The good news: there's a way out, and it's usually in this order.

## The order that works

1. **Get online on day one.** A travel eSIM or pocket Wi-Fi is fine for maps and messages. It's temporary.
2. **Register your address at city hall.** Your residence card needs your address on the back before most phone companies or banks will deal with you.
3. **Get a phone number** with a plan that accepts your overseas credit card (more on this below).
4. **Open your bank account** using that number. (Here's [how the bank side works]({{ site.url }}{% post_url 2026-11-17-bank-account-six-month-rule %}).)
5. **Later, switch to a cheaper plan if you want.** You can keep the same number.

Doing it in this order means you only hit each counter once.

## The number you need (and the one you don't)

Not every phone number counts. Banks, delivery companies and apps like LINE and PayPay usually want a regular mobile number starting with **070, 080 or 090**, and many need it to receive text messages (SMS) for verification.

<mark>A data-only SIM doesn't give you a phone number at all.</mark> Some apps give you a number starting with 050, but many banks and services don't accept 050 numbers.

So when you sign up, ask for a voice SIM (<span class="jp">音声通話SIM</span> *onsei tsūwa shimu*). It's the plan that comes with a real mobile number and SMS.

## Your options

| Type | Examples | Good for | Watch for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Big three carriers | docomo, au, SoftBank | English-speaking staff at big stores, buying a new phone in installments | Most expensive monthly plans |
| Carriers' budget brands | ahamo, povo, LINEMO, UQ mobile, Y!mobile | Same networks, much cheaper | Many are online-only, and some need a Japanese-issued card |
| Budget providers (MVNOs) and foreigner-focused providers | Many, including services aimed at foreign residents | Low prices, some accept overseas credit cards and support English | Slower speeds at busy times on some providers |
| Rakuten Mobile | Rakuten Mobile | Simple pricing | Coverage can be weaker in some buildings and rural areas |

If you don't have a Japanese bank account or a Japan-issued credit card yet, <mark>your first plan will usually be the one that accepts your overseas credit card.</mark> Check that before you go to the shop or start an online sign-up. You can switch to a cheaper plan once your bank account is open.

## What to bring

- Residence card (<span class="jp">在留カード</span> *zairyū kādo*) with your address on the back
- Passport
- A credit card in your own name (and ask in advance whether an overseas card is OK)
- Your phone, if you're bringing your own (check it isn't locked to a carrier in your home country)

<div class="tip" markdown="1">
<strong class="label">Tip<span class="c">:</span></strong> If you already have a phone, you don't need to buy a new one. Say <span class="jp">SIMだけ契約したいです</span> (*Shimu dake keiyaku shitai desu*, "I just want a SIM") so staff don't spend an hour showing you new phones.
</div>

## About those "2-year contracts"

If you've read older guides, you might have heard that Japanese phone contracts lock you in for two years with a big cancellation fee. That's mostly outdated. Since rule changes in 2019, cancellation fees on plans have largely disappeared or are very small.

What still exists is **buying a phone in installments**, often over 24 to 48 months, sometimes with a deal where you return the phone partway through. That's a loan, so there's a credit check. If your visa has only a short time left, you may be turned down for the installment plan even if the SIM contract itself is fine.

## When you leave Japan

- Cancel your contract before you go. Unpaid bills can follow you, including when you come back to Japan.
- If you bought the phone in installments, check how much is left to pay.

<div class="warning" markdown="1">
<strong class="label">Watch out<span class="c">:</span></strong> Never give or sell your SIM card, or a phone contract in your name, to someone else, even a friend who's staying in Japan. Passing a SIM to another person without the carrier's permission is illegal in Japan, and SIMs handed over this way often end up used in scams.
</div>

## Why Japanese people never mention it

Most Japanese people got their first phone through a family plan, paid from a parent's bank account. They've never had to prove anything to get a number. When they say "just go to the phone shop," they genuinely don't know about the loop.

## Quick answers about phones in Japan

**Can I use a prepaid SIM long-term?**
Prepaid SIMs are mostly aimed at tourists and are usually data-only. For a phone number you can use with banks and apps, you'll want a voice SIM.

**Do I lose my number if I switch providers?**
No. Japan has number portability (MNP). You can keep your number when you move to another provider.

**Is eSIM available?**
Yes, most carriers and many budget providers offer eSIM. Check your phone supports it.

**My visa is short. Will I be refused?**
For the SIM itself, usually not. For buying a phone in installments, a short visa can be a problem. Bringing your own phone avoids that.
