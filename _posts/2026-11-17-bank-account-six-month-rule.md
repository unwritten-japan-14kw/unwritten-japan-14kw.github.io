<div class="scene" markdown="1">
Your first week in Japan. You've got your residence card, your new address, and a job starting Monday. You walk into a big-name bank to open an account so you can get paid.

The clerk looks at your card, checks a date, and bows apologetically: "I'm very sorry. Six months."
</div>

You didn't do anything wrong. You just ran into one of the most common walls new arrivals hit. Here's why it happens and how to get around it.

## Where Japan's 6-month bank rule comes from

Japan's foreign exchange law sorts people into "residents" and "non-residents." A foreigner usually counts as a resident after living in Japan for six months. Until then, many banks treat you as a non-resident, and plenty of them simply don't open accounts for non-residents.

Two things make this more confusing:

- Under that same law, <mark>people who work for a company or office in Japan generally count as residents from day one.</mark> So if you're employed here, the six months shouldn't apply to you.
- Banks also have their own internal rules, mostly to stop fraud and money laundering. Some keep the six-month line for everyone, whatever the law says.

So the honest answer is: it depends on the bank, and sometimes on the branch. A "no" at one counter doesn't mean "no" everywhere.

## Which banks in Japan are easier for foreigners

These are general patterns, not guarantees. Policies change, and branches differ.

- **Japan Post Bank** (<span class="jp">ゆうちょ銀行</span> *Yūcho Ginkō*) is the bank most new arrivals end up with. It has branches in post offices almost everywhere and is often more willing to open accounts early, especially if you can show a job or a school.
- **The bank your employer uses.** Many companies pay salaries through one bank and have a relationship with a nearby branch. That branch may already be used to opening accounts for new foreign staff.
- **Big city banks and online banks** are often stricter about time in Japan. They're worth trying later, once you've been here six months.

<strong class="label">Tip<span class="c">:</span></strong> Before you go anywhere, ask your HR department: "Which bank and which branch do other foreign employees use?" One question can save you three wasted trips.
</div>

## Documents you need to open a bank account in Japan

Bring everything on this list, even if the website says you only need some of it. Being turned away for one missing paper is very common.

| Bring | Why |
|---|---|
| Residence card (<span class="jp">在留カード</span> *zairyū kādo*) with your address on the back | Your main ID. The address must already be registered at city hall. |
| Passport | Backup ID. |
| Proof of work or study | A certificate of employment or an employment contract from your company, or a student ID. This is often what turns a "no" into a "yes." |
| A Japanese phone number | Most banks require one. (See the catch below.) |
| A personal seal (*inkan*), if you have one | Many banks now accept a signature, but some branches still ask. |

Banks also check how long your visa has left. If your residence card expires in the next few months, expect more questions.

## The bank–phone catch

Here's the trap: <mark>many banks want a Japanese phone number, and many phone companies want a Japanese bank account or credit card.</mark>

Ways people get out of it:

- Some phone plans accept an overseas credit card. Get a number with one of those first, then go to the bank.
- Your employer may be able to give you a company phone number to list temporarily. Ask HR.
- Japan Post Bank branches are sometimes more flexible about this too.

We'll cover phone contracts in their own guide.

## What to say at the bank counter

Most bank staff won't speak much English, and that's fine. Showing this on your phone is enough to start:

<span class="jp">口座を開設したいです</span> *Kōza o kaisetsu shitai desu.* (I'd like to open an account.)

## Closing your bank account when you leave Japan

<div class="warning" markdown="1">
<strong class="label">Watch out<span class="c">:</span></strong> Never sell or give away your bank account or cash card when you leave Japan, even to a friend. Handing over an account is a crime in Japan, and foreign residents have been arrested for it. Accounts sold this way often end up used in scams. Close your account at the bank before you go.
</div>

## Why Japanese people never mention it

Most Japanese people opened their first account as kids, often with a parent at the local post office. They've never had to prove how long they've lived anywhere. Ask a Japanese coworker which bank to use and they'll probably just name theirs, without knowing you might be turned away.

## Quick answers about bank accounts in Japan

**I was refused once. Should I try another branch?**
Yes. Branch staff interpret rules differently. Bring proof of work or study and try again, ideally at the branch your company recommends.

**Can I get paid without a Japanese bank account?**
Talk to your employer straight away. Some can pay the first salary another way until your account is ready.

**Will I need a new account when I've been here six months?**
No. Once you have one, keep it. You can add a second bank later if you want online banking or English support.

**Do I need to tell the bank when I move or renew my visa?**
Yes. Update your address and residence card details with the bank. Out-of-date details can get your account frozen.
